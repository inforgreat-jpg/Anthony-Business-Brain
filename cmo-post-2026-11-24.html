The post
I saw my first child graduate from a school I owned, and I had to be honest about what it cost to get there. I bought my first preschool in Singapore because I couldn't find one I liked for my own kid, and over the next six years I bought four more and built a team of about 50, all of them women, with me the only man in the room learning from people who knew far more about children than I ever would. The exit was a strategic sale. Revenue had grown more than 10x. On paper, it was the best result of my career. But standing there watching my child walk across that little stage, the number I kept coming back to wasn't on any spreadsheet. Every hour I put into those schools came out of an hour somewhere else. Home. My wife. My kids. Me. I told myself it was an investment, I believed it, and on some days I wasn't sure I was right. I share this not to solicit any sense of sympathy or envy. Rather, the key takeaway is that we count the cost of a venture in dollars and the cost of a career in titles, and almost never in hours. Lying on my death bed, I won't regret the multiple I sold at. I'll regret how I spent my time. The graduation was the return. The exit was just the receipt. Anyone can choose. Here's what I did: I now ask whether the hours are worth it before I ask whether the deal is. This journey has been anything but linear. P.S. What's the one moment you wouldn't trade for any title?
