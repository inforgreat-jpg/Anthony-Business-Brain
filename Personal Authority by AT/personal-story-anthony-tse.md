# PERSONAL STORY DOCUMENT
## Anthony Tse

---

## SECTION 1: ORIGIN & FOUNDATION

### The Moment That Changed Everything
Escaped a car accident at the age of 16 where I saw my friend die at the scene but I was fortunate enough to have escaped the same car accident made me realize that life is fleeting and I want to make the most out of it not having to regret on my deathbed.

### The Person Who Shaped Them
My father helped me realize how fortunate I am and that as a generation I'm already much better than the previous generation so I have nothing to lose and I should just go for it. That helped me become an entrepreneur and for my risk-taking ability.

### The Value Running Their Decisions
Time is limited. Is how I spend my time, energy, and resources aligned with my values and purpose?

---

## SECTION 2: CHARACTER UNDER PRESSURE

### The Hardest Chapter
Hedge Fund: when my shareholders were involved in a shareholder dispute on the side, I had to steer the ship forward but yet was stuck in a stalemate that I knew was difficult to get out of. Yet I had to ensure I looked after my stakeholders, my employees, as well as those who believed in me.

It's a situation where it was generally out of my control, which is why I felt really frustrated. I had to come up with something where I could finally be in control and that's when I decided to liquidate the business via a lawsuit to move on with my life.

### The Risk That Defined Them
I was in Germany in my twenties driving. Like most people they drive very fast in Germany. I tried overtaking a car on a highway, so not an expressway. It was only one lane and as I was trying to overtake the car in front of me I then realized I wasn't going fast enough. As I was just about to pass the car in front of me there was another car coming on the opposite side, so oncoming traffic. It was so close that we almost had a head-on collision and for sure would have died. I swerved back into my lane just as I passed the car in front of me and I was going so fast and so swiftly that the cars worked a little bit.

### The Mistake That Built Them
At 30 and running it for six years hurt me financially because I gave up a lot of income to start a business previously this business ended up in a shareholder dispute where I had to sue the company in order to extricate myself from any liabilities but ultimately what that taught me was the ability to move on independently, the ability for me to survive and achieve financial freedom without relying on an income, without climbing the corporate ladder. Yes while it may have cost me financially in terms of the dollars, I would have my bank account at the end of the day. I became financially free, mentally free, before most people. What this taught me is that life is not always a straight line and that taking detours actually could be healthy and could lead you to something better.

### Standing Their Ground
Say my marriage: I told my wife that certain things were non-negotiable and I would stand firm on things like temper-wise and speaking up loudly, something I just don't accept. I basically said, "Yes I'm happy to have a divorce if certain things are reached," and that caused my wife to go back and rethink our relationship. She went to seek counseling and then our marriage improved significantly after that. When I made that statement it wasn't meant to be a threat. I meant to carry it out so I was putting a lot on the line because if she refuses to change then yes that would be the end of it.

## SECTION 3: THE WORK & THE WHY

### When They Knew
So every day I would wake up with excitement. I look forward to checking my schedule for the upcoming schedule instead of dreading it. I look forward to it. I actually want to prepare for my coaching sessions, for my training sessions. Most importantly after these sessions when I get feedback from clients, they give me positive feedback and tell me how I made a real impact on their lives. This aligns with my values of purpose and living with no regrets.

### What They Do Differently
Anybody who's been there, done that. By the way while I may have succeeded in many people's eyes, I've also failed many times. It's the fact that I failed and made mistakes that makes me valuable. More importantly the fact that I'm very open about my mistakes makes me unique because many people just like to share the positives and successes.

In terms of the mechanics I use a lot of anecdotes in my coaching and training sessions. I don't use theory. I absolutely hate using theory or research. I use empirical evidence and a lot of it comes from me personally. If I'm not able to come up with a personal experience, I'm good at coming up with some kind of analogy to help my client relate their problem or challenges to something more tangible in simple terms.

### The Reframe That Changes Everything
My clients have difficulty firing people or giving them a negative review and I'm able to help them frame it in a positive manner. My client would see laying off somebody because they're not fit for the job as a positive because they're doing this person a favor by reminding them that their time and energy are better spent doing something else. This is not the right job for them. It's better to know now than later after wasting more time. My client feels that this message that's being passed on is, yes, he's actually being a good messenger. Typical example of turning something negative and daunting into positive.

### The Work That Made Them Proud
Building my preschool via acquisitions made a real difference because when I speak to my clients who are parents, they stay with me for years, usually four years. They come back and give raving reviews about how great this class, school, the experience, and curriculum were and how much their kids have grown and also have grown better than other kids. This shows that my curriculum truly is more holistic and better than other people's. Process: I feel really fulfilled and also made good money so I can't think of any other way to live a fulfilling life which is to get paid while making a positive impact on others' lives.

## SECTION 4: WORLDVIEW & DIRECTION

### What Everyone Gets Wrong
Don't have a system? They don't plan? As in they don't think about a business with a structure, with repeatable processes. Many people are shooting in the dark, hoping that the more darts they throw out, the more they'll get. This keeps happening because many people are not business-minded people. They may be good at what they do, such as a great speaker, great coach, but it doesn't mean they know how to build a business. They don't know how to build a brand for themselves.

### How Success Changed Shape
Great question. My definition of success has changed a lot over the years. When I was young, in my 20s, I used to think success was based on: work title, salary, types of conferences one attends, whether you fly business class or five-star hotels, what kind of membership rewards you have.

As I grew older I actually thought success means fewer air miles because you fly less, fewer perks from airlines and hotels, again because you also travel less. More importantly the title doesn't matter anymore because a title only also means that you are supposed to spend a certain amount of time fulfilling a certain role. Now my definition of success is merely about whether the time and energy I'm spending is on work that aligns with my values and purpose. Ultimately what I value is impact, whether or not I'm making someone's life better and in the process being paid for it. That knowing that one day when I die I'm not going to look back and regret on the work that I'm doing. And most importantly at the end of all this is when I share my story people love to hear it. They always tell me, "Wow how come you're able to do this? Wow you have the courage to do that. How are you able to do it?" I'm ultimately able to craft my own story according to my own terms, which is not measured by money and that's how I define success. What changed my mind was being laid off twice and then founding two companies.

### Where This Is All Going
I think this industry is moving more towards using technology and AI. When it comes to coaching then what that means is coaches who are merely certified without real substance, experience, or who have failed and succeeded are going to lose out. They're not gonna have an edge. Most coaches now also need to be mentors and trainers because technology can replace a lot of questioning or asking powerful questions.

### What People Actually Get
The change is that my client will feel more intelligent in terms of learning about businesses and how to actually lead people but then they also realize that they themselves are a lot more capable than they originally thought through my coaching and training they can look back at our coaching sessions and apply it to whatever challenges or roadblocks they are facing. They will say, "Hey I remember this from Anthony's coaching session. I'm able to overcome this because of the fact that there's always something positive from this roadblock and that I'm able to convert this into an inspiring story that I can craft on my own terms."

## SECTION 5: THE PRESCHOOL ROLLUP — VERIFIED TIMELINE & DETAILS
*(Source: WLD podcast interview transcript, "Preschool roll up transcript with WLD.docx" — verified against Anthony's own words, 2026-07-16. This supersedes any prior shorthand version of this story for content-writing purposes.)*

**Real timeline (do not compress):** Laid off from private equity/hedge fund job at 30 (early 2009) → started own hedge fund, Pangu Capital Limited, ran it ~6 years → joined an established hedge fund → relocated to London (~2 years, managing an Asia portfolio on a brutal 8-hour-offset nocturnal schedule) → that firm shut down, laid off again around age 37 → moved to Singapore, worked ~2 years at a single family office → began acquiring preschools in 2018 at age 40. There is no direct "walked away from the hedge fund into preschools" jump — several career chapters sit in between.

**Why preschools, specifically:** Not a grand strategic plan. His first child had just been born (he was 39, a late first-time father) and he didn't like any of the preschools he found in Singapore. He wanted to raise his kid on his own philosophy but didn't know how to homeschool, so he decided to buy and run his own school instead. *(Separately reserved for a future post: he was also professionally unfulfilled in his job at the time and it didn't align with his values — keep this as its own beat, don't merge it into the "preschool for my kid" story.)*

**Buy vs. build reasoning:** Chose acquisition over building from scratch deliberately — as someone with zero prior experience in education, buying meant inheriting an existing client base, staff, and a financial history he could model and project from. His actual words to the inherited staff: *"You're all my mentors, you're my teachers, I'm just here to learn from all of you."*

**Structure at peak:** 5 schools total — first 3 acquired, 4th built from scratch ("greenfield," to prove to himself he could), 5th also acquired. Roughly one per year over five years. When he sold, he sold 4 of the 5 together as a group (the 5th had already been separately divested beforehand).

**What actually changed after acquiring a school:** The academic curriculum (phonics, PinYin, math) stayed the same at first and improved gradually over time — he didn't touch it initially since that's part of why he bought an established school. What changed almost immediately was the philosophy: letting kids fail a worksheet before correcting them, letting them fall rather than hovering, teaching the concept of money starting at age 4 (delayed gratification, needs vs. wants, donating birthday-goodie-bag money to charity instead of buying goodie bags). Teacher buy-in was hard the first year (pushback: "you're not from this industry, you're not certified") until parents bought into the philosophy first, which then brought teachers around.

**The one that failed:** One of the five schools had a toxic culture he could not fix, even after replacing staff one-for-one — incoming staff kept getting absorbed into the existing culture. Lesson he draws from it: you can buy a client base and systems, you cannot buy culture, it's already there (good or broken) the moment you sign.

**The value-creation playbook:** Bought at low single-digit EBITDA multiples. Found many acquired schools running on outdated, non-digitized processes (literally passing around USB drives/discs between staff) — digitizing and centralizing procurement were easy early wins. Raised school fees where the value already justified it (schools were underpriced relative to what they now offered); because most costs in a preschool are fixed, fee increases dropped heavily to the bottom line. Revenue across the group grew more than 10x (confirmed figure, corroborated in [[brand-positioning-anthony-tse]] and business-inbox-anthony-tse.md).

**The exit:** Found the buyer himself through his own private equity network, no broker involved. Confirmed a profitable exit. The exact multiple achieved on sale is not to be disclosed publicly — do not state a specific sale multiple in any content.

**Team:** His organization was 100% women; he was the only man in it. This was a significant adjustment coming from finance, which he describes as male-dominated and aggressive by comparison.
